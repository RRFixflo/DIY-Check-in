General information for landlords in England and Wales as at October 2026, not legal advice. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own rules; check gov.uk or a qualified adviser for your situation.

A privately rented home in England and Wales must have a valid EPC rated E or above before you can start or continue a tenancy, unless you have registered a valid exemption. An EPC lasts 10 years and must be available to prospective tenants free of charge. The government has confirmed a higher standard, the equivalent of an EPC C, for all private tenancies by 1 October 2030.

Do landlords need an EPC?

Yes. You need a valid Energy Performance Certificate when you advertise a home to let, and you should give the tenant a copy at the start of the tenancy. The rating should appear in adverts. An EPC is valid for 10 years from the date it is lodged, and you can check any home’s certificate, rating and expiry date on the EPC register.

A few homes don’t need one, for example some listed buildings (where the works needed would unacceptably alter their character), and individual rooms let in a shared house where the tenant rents a room rather than the whole home. If you let the whole house, it needs an EPC.

What is the minimum EPC rating for rental property?

Under the Minimum Energy Efficiency Standard (MEES) regulations, the minimum is E. Since 1 April 2020 this has applied to every private tenancy in England and Wales, not just new ones. If your home is rated F or G you must not let it until it is improved to E, or until you register an exemption.

Read the government’s minimum energy efficiency standard guidance for landlords for the full rules.

How much do I have to spend? The cost cap

Today, if you can’t reach E, you must make all the recommended improvements you can up to a cap of £3,500 per property (including any grant funding you use). If the home still isn’t at E after that, you can register a “high cost” exemption.

EPC exemptions and the PRS Exemptions Register

An exemption only counts once it is entered on the government’s PRS Exemptions Register. Most last 5 years and don’t pass to a new owner. The main ones are:

  • All relevant improvements made: you’ve done everything that can be done within the cap and it is still below E.
  • High cost: the cheapest recommended improvement would cost more than £3,500.
  • Wall insulation: an expert advises that insulation would damage the building.
  • Consent: a tenant, lender, freeholder or planning authority refuses consent to the work.
  • Devaluation: a surveyor says the work would reduce the property’s value by more than 5%.
  • New landlord: a temporary 6-month exemption in some cases when you have just become the landlord.

You will need evidence for each one (quotes, a surveyor’s report, written refusals), so keep them with your records.

What are the penalties?

Councils enforce MEES. Under the current rules they can fine up to £5,000 per property per breach, with lower bands for shorter breaches and for false information on the register, and they can publish the breach.

EPC C by 2030: the higher standard

In January 2026 the government published its response to the 2025 consultation on raising standards in private rented homes. As reported in that response:

  • All private tenancies in England and Wales are to meet the equivalent of EPC C by 1 October 2030, with one deadline for new and existing tenancies (the earlier idea of 2028 for new tenancies was dropped).
  • EPCs are moving to new headline metrics (such as energy cost, fabric performance and the heating system), replacing the single A to G score used now.
  • A home already rated C on a current-style certificate lodged before 1 October 2029 is expected to count until that certificate expires or is replaced.
  • The cost cap is to stay at £10,000 per property, with a lower cap for cheaper homes, and the maximum fine is to rise to £30,000.

These are planned changes. The detail is set in regulations, so check gov.uk before relying on any figure, and don’t treat a C target as law until it is in force. The current legal minimum is still E.

How to improve your EPC rating

The recommendations page of your EPC lists the improvements and their typical effect. Common, cheaper steps are:

  • Loft insulation topped up to around 270 mm.
  • Cavity wall insulation where the walls suit it.
  • LED lighting throughout.
  • A modern condensing boiler and heating controls (a programmer, room thermostat and thermostatic radiator valves).
  • Hot water cylinder insulation and draught-proofing.
  • Double glazing or secondary glazing.

Get a new EPC after the work, or the improvements won’t show on the register. You can book an EPC for £120 on our certificates page, and see your home’s current rating in a free property report for any UK postcode.

EPCs and your inventory

An EPC records things an inventory can back up: the boiler, the heating controls, insulation you can see in the loft, the type of glazing. Photographing them at the check-in, with the date and time on each photo, shows what was in place and when. A DIY Inventory report also records the meters with photos, which helps if energy use is ever questioned.

Track your EPC expiry

An EPC that expires during a tenancy doesn’t have to be renewed until you next let or sell the home, but it must be valid when you do. Scotland and Northern Ireland

  • Scotland: there is no minimum EPC rating for private lets in force yet. The Scottish Government has consulted on draft regulations for a reformed EPC band C, phased in from 2028 for new lets; check gov.scot for the final version.
  • Northern Ireland: landlords need an EPC to let, but there is no minimum rating for private tenancies.

Questions

What is the minimum EPC rating to rent out a property?

In England and Wales the legal minimum is E, for new and existing tenancies, unless a valid exemption is registered. The government plans to raise this to the equivalent of C by 1 October 2030.

Can I rent out a property with an EPC rating of F or G?

Not unless you register a valid exemption on the PRS Exemptions Register, for example because you have spent the £3,500 cap and it is still below E. Otherwise you can be fined up to £5,000.

How long does a landlord EPC last?

10 years. It must be valid when you advertise and let the home, and you can check its expiry on the EPC register.

Will rented homes need an EPC C by 2030?

That is the government’s confirmed plan: the equivalent of C for all private tenancies by 1 October 2030, with a £10,000 cost cap. It is not yet the legal minimum, so check gov.uk for the regulations.

Do I need an EPC for a room in a shared house?

Usually not for a single room let in a shared house, but the whole building needs one if it is let or sold as a whole. Check the government guidance for your situation.
