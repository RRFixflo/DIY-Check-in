General information for landlords in England as at October 2026, not legal advice. The Renters’ Rights Act applies to England only; Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own tenancy laws. Check gov.uk or a qualified adviser for your situation.

The Renters’ Rights Act 2025 changed private renting in England from 1 May 2026: section 21 “no fault” evictions ended, almost all tenancies became rolling (periodic) tenancies, rent can rise only once a year through a formal notice, and rent in advance is capped at one month. A national landlord register is planned to open region by region from December 2026, and a landlord ombudsman later. Here is what changed, what is still to come, and what it means for your paperwork.

Is section 21 abolished?

Yes. Since 1 May 2026 you can’t use a section 21 notice, even if the tenancy agreement mentions one. Notices served before then had a short deadline for court claims, which has now passed in most cases. To end a tenancy you now need a ground for possession under section 8, such as:

  • Selling the property (ground 1A) or you or close family moving in (ground 1): 4 months’ notice, and not in the first 12 months of the tenancy.
  • Serious rent arrears (ground 8): at least 3 months’ rent owed (13 weeks for weekly rent) when the notice is served and at the hearing, with 4 weeks’ notice.
  • Antisocial behaviour, breach of the tenancy, or damage to the property: discretionary grounds, where the court decides.

Tenants can still leave with 2 months’ notice at any time.

Periodic tenancies: no more fixed terms

Existing assured shorthold tenancies became assured periodic tenancies on 1 May 2026, and new tenancies can’t have a fixed end date. A tenancy now carries on until the tenant gives notice or you gain possession on a ground. For new tenancies you must give the key terms in writing, and existing tenants with a written agreement had to be given the government’s Information Sheet by 31 May 2026.

Rent increases under the Renters’ Rights Act

  • Once a year at most, using a section 13 notice (Form 4A) with at least 2 months’ notice.
  • Rent review clauses in agreements no longer work.
  • Tenants can challenge an increase at the First-tier Tribunal, which can’t set a rent above the market rate.
  • Adverts must show a set asking rent, and you can’t ask for or accept offers above it.
  • Rent in advance is capped at one month, payable once the agreement is signed. Deposit caps (5 weeks’ rent in most cases) are unchanged.

Pets, benefits and children

Tenants can ask to keep a pet, and you must consider the request and answer in writing, normally within 28 days. You can only refuse for a good reason. You also can’t refuse someone, or advertise that you won’t let to them, because they receive benefits or have children.

The landlord database and ombudsman

Every private landlord will need to register themselves and each property on a new government database, with a fee. Trade bodies report that the service opens on 15 December 2026, starting with the West Midlands, with other regions following over the next year. Check gov.uk for your region’s date before relying on it. A landlord ombudsman, which tenants can complain to, is expected to require membership from 2028.

Fines and enforcement

Councils can now fine landlords up to £7,000 for many breaches (such as using a section 21 or discriminating against tenants) and up to £40,000 for serious or repeated ones, and tenants can claim rent repayment orders in more situations. What the Act means for inventories

With no fixed end dates, tenancies run longer and the check-in may be years old by the time a tenant leaves. Possession for damage, and deposit deductions, still depend on showing what changed. That makes the record more important:

  • Do a detailed check-in inventory with dated photos, signed by the tenant.
  • Do regular mid-tenancy inspections, giving at least 24 hours’ written notice, so problems are spotted early and fair wear and tear is easy to judge over a long tenancy.
  • At the end, compare the check-out with the check-in item by item.

Questions

When did section 21 end in England?

Section 21 “no fault” evictions ended on 1 May 2026, when the main parts of the Renters’ Rights Act came into force. Landlords now need a section 8 ground to end a tenancy.

Can landlords still have fixed-term tenancies?

No. Since 1 May 2026 private tenancies in England are assured periodic tenancies with no end date. Tenants can leave with 2 months’ notice.

How often can a landlord raise the rent now?

Once a year, using a section 13 notice (Form 4A) with at least 2 months’ notice. The tenant can challenge it at the First-tier Tribunal.

When does the landlord database start?

It is planned to open region by region, reported to start on 15 December 2026 in the West Midlands. Check gov.uk for your area’s date and the fee.

Does the Renters’ Rights Act apply in Wales or Scotland?

No, it applies to England. Wales has the Renting Homes (Wales) Act 2016 and Scotland has private residential tenancies, each with their own rules.

Do I still need an inventory under the Renters’ Rights Act?

An inventory isn’t a legal requirement, but it is still the main evidence for deposit deductions and damage claims. Longer tenancies make dated check-in photos and regular inspections more useful.
